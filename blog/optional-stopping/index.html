Probability

Optional stopping, without the magic trick

A stopping rule can change when we observe a fair process. Under the right conditions, it does not change the process’s expected value.

August 19, 2026 · 5 minute read

Suppose a game is fair after every round: given everything observed so far, the expected next change is zero. It is tempting to conclude that any strategy for deciding when to quit must also be fair. That intuition is useful, but incomplete.

The intuition

A stopping rule uses information already available. It may say “stop after the first win” or “stop after ten rounds,” but it cannot use tomorrow’s coin flip. If the process is a martingale and the stopping time is suitably controlled, optional stopping formalizes the claim that timing alone does not create expected profit.

E[M_T] = E[M_0]

Why the conditions matter

The theorem is not permission to attach an arbitrary stopping time to any martingale. Common sufficient conditions bound the stopping time, bound the process, or impose uniform-integrability conditions. Without control, rare paths can carry enough probability mass to break the conclusion.

A finite example

Consider a fair random walk that stops after the first step or after a fixed finite horizon. Because the horizon is bounded, the stopped process remains well behaved. Branching over the possible first step changes the distribution of outcomes, but not their mean.

Main takeaway. Optional stopping is a theorem about information and integrability, not a loophole for converting a fair game into a favorable one.

A market interpretation

The same caution applies to backtests. A rule that exits after a threshold is not automatically suspect, but selecting the rule after inspecting the whole path can introduce future information. The mathematical distinction between a stopping time and hindsight is also a useful research-design distinction.
